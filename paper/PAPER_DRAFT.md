**Abstract**—Generic running plans rarely account for an individual runner's current fitness, schedule, and limitations, while unconstrained large language models (LLMs) may produce training plans whose quality varies and whose numerical content is not guaranteed to be consistent with the runner profile. This study explores a hybrid recommendation system that generates personalized, multi-week running plans while keeping physiological quantities under deterministic control. The proposed architecture first validates a runner profile, then computes a VDOT-based fitness index and training pace zones, and generates a rule-based baseline plan using domain-specific constraints for weekly volume progression, long-run share, cutback weeks, and tapering. An LLM is then used only as a personalization layer over the full multi-week schedule: it receives the complete baseline and returns a week-by-week plan whose explanatory text may be rewritten, while a server-side validator checks every week and rejects any output that alters phases, workout types, distances, paces, intensity zones, weekly mileage, or training-day counts. The production path falls back to a deterministic synthesizer when the LLM output is rejected, whereas the research path reports each generation's outcome without substitution. The prototype is implemented with FastAPI, SQLite, and React, supports plan durations of 4 to 24 weeks, and links planned workouts with logged workouts. The backend test suite of 221 automated tests passes. ## I. Introduction

Regular endurance exercise is widely promoted for health and performance, but the training stimulus a runner needs depends on current fitness, available training days, goal distance, and injury history. Printed or online plans are typically generic templates, and tailoring them requires coaching expertise that many recreational runners do not have. Personalization is the central promise of recommender systems, which adapt content to individual users [1], and its quality is usually judged not only by accuracy but by the user's perceived experience [2], [3].

Large language models (LLMs) have recently been proposed as components of recommender systems because of their world knowledge, flexible text generation, and ability to reason over natural-language user descriptions [4], [5]. They have been adapted to recommendation through instruction tuning [6], unified text-to-text formulations [7], and zero-shot ranking [8]. In the exercise domain, several studies have prompted general-purpose chatbots to generate exercise recommendations or training plans and have judged them against guidelines or coaching-expert criteria [9], [10], including six-week running plans generated by ChatGPT [11] and resistance-training plans from different LLMs [12]. Their findings point to variability: plan quality depended on how much input information was supplied [11], and ratings differed between LLMs even though repeated identical prompts yielded reproducible plans [12]. A scoping review reports heterogeneous and often low-rigor evaluation designs [13], and a systematic review of 24 empirical studies reported safety flaws in 14 of them and LLM-generated plans that were inferior to expert plans in most head-to-head trials of physiological adaptation [14]. One exploratory expert review of plans for fictional patients with type 2 diabetes likewise reported serious safety issues in some plans [15].

These concerns are not incidental. LLMs are known to generate plausible but nonfactual content [16], and a training prescription in which a distance, pace, or weekly volume is silently altered can be inconsistent with the runner's profile. Training science treats volume progression [17], [18], intensity distribution [19], [20], [21], and tapering [22], [23] as quantities to be managed deliberately, not as free text. One response is to constrain LLMs with domain knowledge: recent work grounds an LLM in a sports-science knowledge graph for training-plan generation [24], and a fine-tuned personal-health model has been evaluated for sleep and fitness coaching from wearable data [25]; recommender research on running has also used runners' training data and explanations [26]. The gap addressed here is therefore not the absence of LLM-generated running plans, which have been studied [11], but the limited evidence, in the sources reviewed, on a design in which deterministic running-domain calculations and training constraints establish a baseline plan before LLM personalization, server-side validation is applied after generation, and different LLMs are compared on the same runner profile and the same baseline plan. This study explores that design. No claim of novelty is made beyond what the cited literature supports [VERIFY: a documented search protocol is required before any novelty statement is submitted].

This paper proposes a hybrid recommendation architecture for personalized multi-week running plans and investigates it through a prototype that serves as the research artifact; the scientific evaluation consists of technical validation, a controlled LLM comparison, and a user evaluation. The prototype does not train a machine-learning model: physiological quantities come from closed-form equations and rule-based constraints, and the LLM is used through an external API for text personalization only. The study is guided by three research questions.

- **RQ1.** How can a hybrid AI-based system generate personalized multi-week running training plans by combining deterministic training constraints with LLM-based personalization?
- **RQ2.** How do different LLMs perform in personalizing a deterministic running training plan while maintaining consistency with the runner profile and predefined training constraints? [EXPERIMENT REQUIRED FOR RQ2]
- **RQ3.** How do users perceive the generated training plans in terms of personalization, usefulness, clarity, and confidence? [DATA REQUIRED]

The objectives are to design and implement the architecture (RQ1), to define and run a controlled comparison of LLMs on the personalization layer (RQ2), and to measure perceived plan quality with a user study (RQ3). The contributions are stated conservatively. (1) A hybrid architecture for personalized running training recommendation. (2) An integration of deterministic running-domain constraints, each classified as literature-informed or as an engineering design constraint, with LLM-based personalization. (3) A server-side validation mechanism, implemented for the personalized first week, that constrains the numerical and structural fields of the generated plan against the baseline. (4) A methodology for comparing LLM personalization under the same runner profile and baseline plan, which is defined but not yet conducted [EXPERIMENT REQUIRED]. (5) A user-centered evaluation design for perceived personalization, usefulness, clarity, and confidence, whose instrument is under development and unvalidated [DATA REQUIRED]. The number of software features is not treated as a research contribution, and no claim is made about physiological outcomes, injury prevention, or clinical validity.

## II. Related Work

### A. Personalized Recommendation Systems

Recommender systems estimate the utility of items for a user and are commonly grouped into content-based, collaborative, and hybrid approaches; a widely cited survey proposed extensions such as richer user and item models, contextual information, and multicriteria ratings [1]. Evaluation has also shifted from offline accuracy to user experience: Knijnenburg et al. model how system characteristics affect perceived quality and experience through subjective constructs [2], and Pu et al. propose a questionnaire-based, user-centric evaluation framework [3]. These works motivate our use of perceived-quality constructs, but they concern item recommendation rather than multi-week exercise prescription, and we do not claim our instrument is derived from them (Section III-J).

### B. LLM-Based Recommender Systems

Surveys describe how LLMs can enter the recommendation pipeline and discuss opportunities and challenges [4], [5]. TALLRec tunes an LLM with recommendation instructions to align it with the task [6]; P5 casts recommendation tasks as text generation with personalized prompts [7]; and Hou et al. study LLMs as zero-shot rankers [8]. These approaches target ranking or next-item prediction over catalogs. Our task differs: the output is a structured, safety-relevant plan in which some fields must remain identical to a computed baseline. In this respect, hallucination surveys are directly relevant, since a survey documents that LLMs generate plausible but nonfactual content and discusses detection and mitigation [16].

### C. LLM-Based Exercise and Physical Activity Recommendation

Early empirical studies prompt general-purpose chatbots and compare the outputs with guidelines or expert criteria. Washif et al. examined ChatGPT-designed 12-week resistance programs against established guidelines and literature [9]. Zaleski et al. scored chatbot exercise recommendations for 26 clinical populations on comprehensiveness, accuracy, and readability using a coding scheme informed by gold-standard recommendations [10]. Akrimi et al., in what they describe as a discussion paper based on preliminary data, had coaching experts review ChatGPT-4o plans for three fictional patients with type 2 diabetes; some plans showed serious safety issues, and more detailed prompts tended to yield better-rated plans [15]. At the evidence-synthesis level, a scoping review of 20 evaluation studies reported heterogeneous strategies and low methodological rigor, with a median rigor score of 2.5 out of 5 [13], and a systematic review of 24 empirical studies reported safety flaws in 14 of them and LLM plans inferior to human experts in 5 of 6 head-to-head trials of physiological adaptation [14].

Running-specific work is most directly represented by Düking et al., who evaluated three six-week running plans generated by ChatGPT with increasing granularity of input information, from a generic request to detailed athlete information. Coaching experts rated each plan on 22 criteria drawn from the literature using a 5-point scale, and the plans were compared with a Friedman test [11]. The plans were rated sub-optimal, plans generated from more input information received higher ratings, and the authors advised against using ChatGPT-generated plans without an expert coach's feedback [11]. Havers et al. compared hypertrophy-related resistance training plans generated by GPT-4 and Google Gemini from two prompts of different detail, had 12 coaching experts rate them, and reported high reproducibility when the same prompts were entered multiple times and higher ratings for GPT-4 on several criteria [12]. At the system level, He et al. propose LLM-SPTRec, an LLM grounded in a sports-science knowledge graph, and report gains over collaborative-filtering, sequential, and general-purpose LLM baselines on plan coherence, goal relevance, and predicted user satisfaction [24]; Khasentino et al. report a fine-tuned personal-health LLM that performed similarly to human experts on fitness case studies [25]; and Feely et al. propose a case-based recommender for marathon runners that reuses the training data of similar runners, presents explanations, and was evaluated offline on a real-world dataset [26].

The present study differs from these works in method rather than in claimed quality. First, the plan is not produced directly by an LLM: a deterministic baseline computed from the runner's 5K personal best and training constraints is produced first, and the LLM personalizes only its explanatory content, whereas the plans in [11] were generated directly by ChatGPT from prompts. Second, the prototype applies programmatic server-side validation after generation; the abstracts of [11], [12], and [24] do not describe such a post-generation check [VERIFY against the full texts]. Third, the planned RQ2 comparison varies the LLM while holding the runner profile and baseline fixed, which is related to the multi-LLM and repeated-prompt design of [12] but applies to running plans and to numerical consistency with a baseline. Fourth, the evaluations in [11] and [12] relied on coaching-expert ratings against literature-derived criteria, whereas the planned user evaluation targets runners' perceived personalization, usefulness, clarity, and confidence; expert ratings are complementary and could be added to RQ2 (Section III-I). These differences concern design, not superiority: the present system has not yet been evaluated, uses an LLM for the first week only, and inherits the limits of its engineering rules (Section V-E).

### D. Running Training and Exercise Prescription

The system's deterministic layer is based on a published performance-index approach in which an index (VDOT) derived from a race result sets training paces [27]. Observational and review evidence describes endurance training as predominantly low intensity with a minority of high-intensity work, commonly summarized as roughly 80%/20% of sessions [19], [20]; a randomized study in well-trained athletes reported the largest gains in key endurance variables for a polarized distribution [21], although the population and duration differ from recreational runners. Progression of weekly volume is often limited to about 10%, yet the evidence is mixed: a randomized trial of a 10%-rule-based graded program found no reduction in running-related injuries in novice runners [18], and a cohort study found no significant difference in injury rates across progression categories overall, with differences for particular injury types [17]. Tapering evidence suggests maintaining intensity while reducing volume [22], and a meta-analysis identified a 2-week taper with exponentially decreasing volume as the most effective strategy among those compared [23]. Gabbett's review frames load management as balancing training stimulus and injury risk [28], and training periodization divides a program into smaller periods and units to structure training, although the traditional model has recognized limitations [29]. Consequently, each rule below is described either as literature-informed, where a source is cited, or as an engineering design constraint used in the proposed system, rather than as an established law (Section III-E).

### E. Evaluation of AI/LLM Recommendations

LLM evaluation surveys stress that evaluation must be organized by what is evaluated, where, and how [30], and the LLM-as-a-judge approach has been proposed as a scalable way to approximate human preferences, with reported position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases and limited reasoning ability [31]. For user studies, Likert-type data are ordinal and the appropriateness of parametric analysis has been debated; parametric methods have been argued to be robust for such data [32]. Internal consistency of multi-item scales is conventionally assessed with Cronbach's alpha [33]. We use these sources to justify analysis choices, not to claim that our instrument is validated.

### F. Research Gap

Existing work has examined LLM-generated exercise and running plans, mostly by expert rating of directly generated text [9], [10], [11], [12], [15]. It has reported variability, dependence on input information, and safety and rigor concerns [13], [14], and it has proposed knowledge-grounded generation [24] and non-LLM running recommenders [26]. This study explores a hybrid design in which (i) deterministic running-domain calculations and training constraints establish a baseline plan, (ii) an LLM personalizes that baseline, and (iii) server-side validation is applied after personalization. If the planned controlled experiment is conducted, it will compare different LLMs using the same runner profile and the same deterministic baseline, and the user evaluation will examine perceived personalization, usefulness, clarity, and confidence. These elements are explored together here; the study does not claim to be the first to address any of them individually. ### C. Input Validation

Input is validated by Pydantic field constraints and validators: time fields must match `MM:SS`; the target race time must match `MM:SS` or `HH:MM:SS`; the race distance is normalized to one of three values; and numeric bounds apply (Table I). Invalid input yields an HTTP 422 response.

### D. Physiological Calculation

For a race of distance $d$ meters completed in $t$ minutes, velocity is $v = d/t$ (m/min). The implementation uses the oxygen-cost and time-dependent fraction equations attributed to Daniels and Gilbert:

$$\dot{V}O_2(v) = -4.60 + 0.182258\,v + 0.000104\,v^{2} \tag{1}$$

$$F(t) = 0.8 + 0.1894393\,e^{-0.012778\,t} + 0.2989558\,e^{-0.1932605\,t} \tag{2}$$

$$\mathrm{VDOT} = \dot{V}O_2(v) / F(t) \tag{3}$$

(`rules/vdot.py: calculate_vdot_from_race`). VDOT is computed from the 5K personal best only ($d=5000$ m). Training paces are obtained by inverting (1) at a target oxygen cost $\dot{V}O_2^{*} = p\cdot\mathrm{VDOT}$, taking the positive root of $0.000104v^{2}+0.182258v-(4.60+\dot{V}O_2^{*})=0$, with $v$ floored at 60 m/min, and converting to seconds per kilometer as $60000/v$. The fractions $p$ implemented are 0.65–0.74 (easy), 0.80 (marathon), 0.88 (threshold), 0.98 (interval), and 1.05 (repetition). These constants are implementation choices labeled with Daniels-style zone names; their agreement with the published intensity definitions [27] should be checked against the source before submission [VERIFY]. The original equations are from a 1979 publication that predates the 2001 reference limit; the source is not cited here pending the authors' approval [PRE-2001 SOURCE — APPROVAL REQUIRED], and the secondary reference [27] is used instead. For the canonical test profile (5K in 24:56), the implementation returns VDOT = 38.43; the test suite asserts only that VDOT lies between 37 and 40. Target race pace is the target time divided by the race distance, and a feasibility class is assigned from the relative pace improvement over the 5K pace using thresholds of 0%, 3.5%, and 6.5%; these thresholds are an engineering design constraint used in the proposed system, and no supporting literature has been identified [VERIFY/JUSTIFY].

### E. Rule-Based Baseline Training Recommendation

The deterministic generator (`rules/generator.py`) applies the rules in Table II. Each rule is classified as literature-informed or as an engineering design constraint used in the proposed system; none is presented as a universal law.

**TABLE II. Training rules implemented in the baseline generator and their classification**

| Rule | Implementation | Classification | Literature context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starting volume | Week 1 target is the current weekly mileage times 1.06 (if base ≥ 20 km) or 1.08, subject to rounding | Engineering design constraint | Limits on weekly progression are debated [17], [18]; the factors themselves have no direct source |
| Progression | Build weeks multiply the volume of the immediately preceding week — whether that week was itself a build week or a recovery cutback week — by 1.06 or 1.08 | Engineering design constraint | Same as above |
| Cutback | Every 4th week (not in the last two) is 0.85 times the last build volume | Engineering design constraint | Periodization in general [29]; no source identified for a 15% reduction every fourth week |
| Taper | Second-to-last week is 0.85 and last week is 0.55 times the base (starting) mileage | Partly literature-informed (volume reduced, intensity retained); magnitudes are engineering constraints | [22], [23] |
| Long run | 30% of the weekly target volume, capped at 12/16/21 km for 5K/10K/half marathon, floored at 4 km (the realized share can exceed 30%; see below) | Engineering design constraint | No source identified |
| Quality session | 20% of weekly volume, floored at 3.5 km; tempo for beginners, interval otherwise | Implementation heuristic loosely motivated by a low/high-intensity split | [19], [20] report shares of sessions, not distance |
| Easy runs | Remaining volume split evenly over remaining training days, floored at 3 km | Implementation heuristic | None |
| Day placement | Long run on Sunday (else Saturday, else last active day); quality on Tuesday/Wednesday/Thursday if active | Implementation heuristic | None |
| Single training day | One easy-paced long-run session carrying the weekly volume | Edge-case handling | None |

The "80/20" label is implemented as a distance-share heuristic: the quality session is about 20% of weekly distance, and long and easy runs are prescribed at easy pace. This differs from the session-count and time-in-zone descriptions in [19], [20], and quality sessions include warm-up distance, so the label should be read as approximate. One property of the implementation follows directly from Table II: taper weeks are anchored to the starting mileage rather than the peak, so for long plans the drop into the taper is large (37% to 55% in the examples of Section V-B). This anchoring is intentional, not an artifact: it keeps the taper's depth predictable from the runner's own baseline rather than from how far a given plan happened to build, and it is the rule actually implemented and tested. An earlier implementation also computed the first build week after a cutback from the pre-cutback volume rather than from the cutback week's own (reduced) volume, producing an unintended ~25% rise relative to the cutback week; this was corrected so that the week after a cutback now rises by the normal ~6% (or ~8%, depending on `current_weekly_mileage`) relative to the cutback week itself, consistent with every other build-to-build step in the schedule (verified by an added regression test suite). Because floors (4, 3.5, and 3 km) and caps are applied after the percentages, and because the volume must be distributed over the available training days, the rules in Table II are design targets, not guarantees about the realized plan. An exhaustive deterministic check of the engine (every duration from 4 to 24 weeks, 1 to 7 training days, five base mileages, three experience levels and three race distances; 6,615 profiles; test file `backend/tests/test_rule_engine_properties.py`) found no exception and confirmed in every case the week structure, the phase schedule, the build, cutback and taper formulas, and build steps of at most 8%. Two properties did not hold in general. First, the long-run share of realized weekly distance can exceed 30%: by up to about 60% with two training days, and in some cases with three to seven training days or low volume. Second, realized weekly distance can depart from the progression target by more than 10%: with two training days it is well below the target in every week (by up to about 70%), and when the number of training days is large relative to the weekly volume it is above the target because of the per-session minimum distances (by up to several times at low base mileage). On the 24 synthetic dry-run profiles (3 to 5 training days, 20 to 40 km per week), 20 of 288 profile-weeks exceeded a 30% long-run share (maximum 36%) and 4 deviated from the target by more than 10% (maximum 50%). These dry-run profiles are not the final study profiles, which are not yet determined, so this result does not show that the final profiles will be affected. The baseline algorithm was not changed in response; studies using the baseline should report realized values rather than assume the design values.

### F. LLM-Based Personalization

[FIGURE TO BE GENERATED]

**Fig. 2.** LLM personalization pipeline: prompt construction from the profile and the full multi-week deterministic baseline, provider call, JSON parsing, per-week validation against the baseline, and fallback to the deterministic synthesizer (production path only).

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the LLM receives a system prompt and a user prompt (`ai/prompts.py`) containing the runner profile, the VDOT, target race pace, pace zones, and the **complete multi-week deterministic baseline** (`rule_based_baseline_plan`, one entry per week). The prompt instructs the model to return one `weeks` entry per baseline week, keeping each week's `week_number`, `phase`, `weekly_mileage_km`, and each workout's `day`, `workout_type`, `distance_km`, `pace_target`, and `intensity_zone` unchanged, and to personalize warm-up and cool-down, execution cues, injury-related adaptations, and explanations, returning a fixed JSON schema. Supported providers are Google Gemini (default model `gemini-1.5-flash`) and OpenAI (default `gpt-4o-mini`), both with temperature 0.4 and JSON-mode output; provider selection is by configuration (`gemini`, `openai`, or `auto`, which tries Gemini first). The default model identifiers must be re-verified for availability at experiment time [VERIFY]. No other providers are implemented. The LLM is therefore not a source of physiological values: all numbers in its input are computed deterministically and the validator checks that they are returned unchanged.

In the production path, if no API key is configured, the API call fails, the JSON cannot be parsed, or validation fails, the system uses a deterministic offline synthesizer that copies the baseline numbers verbatim for every week and fills the textual fields from templates. This fallback is not an LLM and must not be described as one; the stored field `ai_model_used` records which path was used, and the production dispatcher marks it internally with status `GEMINI_FALLBACK`. The research path (`generate_gemini_result`) never falls back: each generation is reported as `GEMINI_SUCCESS`, `GEMINI_VALIDATION_FAILED` (with the raw parsed output or the raw non-JSON text preserved for analysis), or `GEMINI_API_ERROR`, with a secret-free error category. The research experiment must call this path directly rather than the production endpoint, so that failures are never masked by substitution.

### G. Server-Side Validation

`validate_ai_plan` (`ai/validator.py`) parses the output with a Pydantic schema and then compares every week with the corresponding week of the deterministic baseline (week count, week number, phase, and per-workout fields, plus weekly mileage and rest/training-day counts for each week). Table III lists what is and is not checked.

| Check | Implemented |
|---|---|

| Workout type unchanged (case-insensitive) | Yes |
| Distance within ±0.05 km of baseline | Yes |
| Pace target string identical | Yes |
| Intensity zone string identical | Yes |
| Weekly mileage within ±0.15 km | Yes |
| Rest-day and training-day counts match the profile | Yes |
| Reordering to Monday–Sunday | Yes |

### H. Multi-Week Plan Generation

**Fig. 3.** Multi-week plan generation workflow: weekly progression, per-week workout distribution, Week 1 personalization, materialization into scheduled workouts, and derivation of calendar dates.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the generator produces one entry per week for the requested duration (4–24 weeks), each with seven days (training or rest), phase label, and weekly mileage; pace zones are constant across weeks and only distances vary. LLM personalization covers every week of this schedule: the model returns a `weeks` entry per baseline week, and the validator checks each week against the baseline. Whether this multi-week personalization is effective is a question for the planned experiment, not something established by the implementation alone. For authenticated users, all weeks are materialized into the `training_plan_workouts` table (7 rows per week, including rest days) at generation time, with the plan start date as the anchor, and each row's calendar date is derived from the start date, week number, and weekday. Anonymous research plans store JSON only and have no scheduled rows. Only one plan per user is active; generating a new plan archives the previous one.

## IV. System Implementation

### A. Technology Stack

The backend uses Python 3.11 with FastAPI, Pydantic for validation, SQLAlchemy for persistence on SQLite, Alembic for schema migrations (three migration versions), `httpx` for LLM API calls, and `slowapi` for rate limiting. The frontend uses React 19, React Router, Vite, and Tailwind CSS. Tests use `pytest`. No machine-learning framework is used.

### B. Architecture and API

The frontend calls a REST API under `/api`. The central endpoint `POST /api/plans/generate` validates the profile, runs the rule engine, requests LLM personalization with validation and fallback, stores the profile and both plan versions as JSON, and (for authenticated users) materializes the schedule. Other endpoints cover authentication (register, login, logout, current user, forgot/reset password), user profile, plan retrieval and listing, plan workouts, workout logging (create, list, read, update, soft delete), a dashboard summary and weekly progress, and research evaluation submission and statistics.

### C. Database and Ownership

Tables are `users`, `user_profiles`, `sessions`, `password_reset_tokens`, `runner_profiles`, `training_plans`, `plan_evaluations`, `training_plan_workouts` (planned workouts), and `workout_logs` (actual workouts, soft-deleted). A plan with a null `user_id` is an anonymous research plan; a user-owned plan is readable only by its owner, and other requests receive the same 404 as a nonexistent plan. Logged workouts can be linked to a planned workout only after a server-side check that the planned workout belongs to the same user.

### D. Authentication and Security

Passwords are hashed with Argon2; sessions use random opaque tokens stored only as SHA-256 hashes, delivered in an HttpOnly cookie with SameSite=Lax and a configurable Secure flag; password reset tokens are single-use, hashed, and time-limited (default 30 minutes); state-changing requests that carry a session cookie must present an allowed Origin or Referer; CORS origins are configured, not wildcard; and rate limits are configured for login, registration, password reset, plan generation, and workout writes. The email provider is not integrated (reset links are logged server side). The research evaluation endpoints do not require authentication [VERIFY whether this is intended for the study].

### E. Planned-versus-Actual Workouts and Progress

Completion status is derived on every read, never stored: a non-rest planned workout is completed if it has a non-deleted linked log, missed if its scheduled date has passed without one, and scheduled otherwise. The dashboard shows the current week of the plan and weekly and monthly totals. Progress insights are deterministic and template-based, comparing the last three completed weeks with the three before them, and produce at most two sentences without scores or LLM calls. These insights are descriptive and are not used to alter plans.

### E. Discussion

Regarding RQ1, the implementation shows that a hybrid design can be realized in which the LLM only receives and returns a baseline whose numerical content is verified by code. The deterministic layer fully determines numbers, and the fallback ensures a plan is always returned. Regarding RQ2 and RQ3, no evidence is available yet, so no conclusion is drawn.

Several limitations affect interpretation. (1) The LLM receives and returns the full multi-week schedule as implemented, but whether its multi-week personalization is effective has not yet been evaluated; no experimental result is reported here, so "personalized multi-week plan" describes the implemented design, not a demonstrated outcome. (2) Validation checks numerical identity but not the safety of generated text, which may include nutrition or injury advice produced without verification; an exploratory expert review reported serious safety issues in some LLM-generated plans [15]. (3) The offline fallback is templated; if it serves as the "AI" arm in a user study, the study compares rule-based text with template text, not an LLM. (4) Age, sex, 10K PB, and easy pace do not change the numbers, and injury information only changes text. (5) The progression and taper constants are heuristics; the 10% progression rationale has mixed evidence [17], [18], the cutback rule has no direct literature source, and the taper is anchored to starting volume. (6) The prototype is not clinically validated, there is no longitudinal outcome measurement, and no claim of improved performance or injury reduction is made; a systematic review reported that LLM-generated plans were inferior to expert plans in most head-to-head trials of physiological adaptation [14], so effectiveness should not be assumed. (7) External LLM APIs may change behavior or be retired, so model identifiers and dates must be reported. (8) The evaluation interface presently pre-fills Likert sliders with non-neutral default values and displays the plan type to participants, which may bias ratings and must be corrected before data collection [NOT IMPLEMENTED: neutral defaults, blinding, participant ID]. (9) The realized long-run share and the realized weekly distance of the baseline can depart from the 30% and progression-target design values for some input combinations (Section III-E); the result on 24 synthetic dry-run profiles does not show that the final, not yet determined, study profiles are affected, and the study must report realized values rather than assume the design values.

## VI. Conclusion

This paper presented a hybrid architecture for personalized running training plans in which deterministic VDOT-based computation and rule-based constraints define the plan, an LLM personalizes explanatory text across the multi-week schedule, and a server-side validator enforces week-by-week numerical consistency with a deterministic fallback in the production path. For RQ1, the implemented prototype generates 4–24-week plans, schedules them, and links planned and logged workouts, and 221 automated tests pass. For RQ2 and RQ3, the evaluation protocol is defined but [RESULTS TO BE COLLECTED], so no conclusion about model differences or user perception can be drawn. The prototype is not a medical device and is not shown to improve performance. Future work includes extending validation to text safety, applying personalization to later weeks, implementing the 16-item instrument with pilot validation, building an LLM comparison harness with stored outputs, running the user study with ethics approval, and revisiting the progression and taper rules against the literature.
